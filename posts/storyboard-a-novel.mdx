Would you look at that? We’re finally in the twenties, and that’s a relief for a number of reasons, not least of which being that I sure could use some spare twenties right about now. It’s been a journey and a half to get to this point, so please avail yourself to the travails stretching back to the [start of the series](/posts/document-everything).

It’s amusing to me that this post directly follows my [*Survivor* audition](/posts/audition-for-survivor) because, as with the *Survivor* tape, this is the first step towards a dream that originated back in childhood. Like it or not, we are neck-deep in the long-term wish fulfillment now.

I’ve wanted to write a book for practically as long as I’ve been able to read. My childhood home is littered with the evidence of countless false starts and dubious premises. I even took a shot at co-writing a novel with one of my classmates in middle school. We were going to alternate chapters and character POVs, high-concept stuff from the mind of an eighth grader who got his hands on *Game of Thrones* just a *little* too early. <span className="text-[#77FFE4] text-sm">[Thanks ~~mother~~ editor!]</span>

It should not be surprising, then, that there’s always been at least one task on the list that would have me revisiting that effort in earnest. In my attempts to recalibrate my efforts in the back half of the list (and [for the year ahead](/post/chart-a-course)), I specifically identified writing as an area I wanted to give particular focus. One of my favorite parts of each task has been writing these posts, and the sheer word count needed to properly document the Endeavor has given me the confidence that I can see my way through the comparatively daunting notion of drafting a full manuscript.

## Best Come Prepared

There are as many competing ideas about the proper level of preparation for writing a novel as authors who have written one. Considering my previous starts all failed to launch, I figured I ought to try out a different tack other than the Stephen King approach of “just write” that led me to jump straight in to my prior attempts and hope I found my way before I lost steam. I’d somehow stumbled across the [Snowflake Method](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/) in the time since I last entertained the notion of writing a book and had made a mental note to test it out when I next had an idea worth fleshing out.

The first thing that the method helped me realize was how little I truly knew about what I wanted to write. Sure, I had a silly premise that helped set the stage, but I was lacking all the functional bits of craft that actually propel the story forward. Little did I know how quickly I would spiral down a Wikipedia rabbit hole on French sculpture and daylight saving, which is alright in my book considering that's precisely the type of obscure tangent that I am wont to go on, prompted or otherwise. 

One of the stumbling blocks that has stopped many a *novel concept* (italicized because I can’t let a pun be subtle) in its tracks has been my proclivity for spending too much time looking for the perfect tool for the job. In all fairness, I don’t think I ever felt *that* passionately about any of those ideas, using them mostly as fodder to justify my burning desire for a [writerdeck](https://www.writerdeck.org) (a.k.a. the most ostentatiously pretentious way to prove oneself a *serious writer*). This time around, I kept it simple and went straight for the consensus best-in-class software, Scrivener, and have had fun learning my way around the program. Turns out, it lends itself well to whatever path you want your novel-writing to take, Wikipedia rabbit holes included, so maybe it’s true that some things are popular for a reason.

There’s still a long way to go before I’m ready to commence writing. Next comes expanding on my character profiles, going deep on background research, and – given the particularities of the novel – sketching out a general timeline of events. I can’t give away too much too soon, especially at a phase where so much is still subject to change. Maybe someday I’ll finally learn the lesson about showing instead of telling, but sometimes the telling is all you’ve got to offer. 

I’ve got my work cut out for me to bring this one to fruition, but if there’s anything that the Endeavor demonstrates (detours, diversion, and all) is that I can set myself on a grand undertaking and work through it bit by bit, even if it is slower than intended much of the time. Sooner or later, I’ll get there. 

One day.
