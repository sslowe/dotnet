## Home is where the studio is

My studio has taken on many forms throughout the years. It started humbly enough, cobbled together out of spare bits of gear my father and brother had acquired over the years. The first piece of equipment that I truly invested in was a quality interface. A good audio interface is the heart of any studio as it's the bit of kit that converts analog inputs from instruments and microphones into digital signals your computer can process. I took what amounted to my entire life savings to that point and bought the interface that I still use to this day, a FireFace UFX, which is by far the longest-lived digital device I’ve ever owned, so the money was well spent in the long run. The first recording I ever made in my “studio” was a cover of Tuesday’s Gone with my brother. He played guitar and sang, and I played drums and mandolin. The engineering wasn’t anything special, but it blew both of our minds that we could make something that sounded so polished with the tools at our disposal.

During my first year in college, my studio dreams were constrained by the floor space afforded me by a freshman dorm room, but that didn’t stop me from cramming in just enough gear to get the job done (and earn more than one noise complaint from the floor RA). Sophomore year, though? I had *big* plans for sophomore year. As a drummer, I was always destined to play host for the purposes of practices and the like but learned that there’s a joy that comes with being the destination for your musical friends. That was precisely what I hoped to foster when I moved off campus and rented a second bedroom for a studio. It was a space that brought numerous people into my life over the next three years and solidified some of the deepest friendships I have to this day.

One of those friendships helped me take my studio to new heights, or at least new latitudes. Cameron Champion, or Cam Champ for short, was the closest thing I had to a partner in crime during my time in Chapel Hill, and we were perennially coming up with new musical schemes, like the not one, but two bands we started together or the student organization we ran for musicians on and off-campus. By far the most hare-brained out of all the projects we cooked up was a road trip over our junior-year spring break consisting of a jaunt to NOLA and back in a [U-Haul outfitted as a mobile recording studio](https://roadtrippers.com/magazine/haulin-sessions-music-road-trip-south/).

The following summer I interned at AWS, and with my newfound paycheck I did what any self-respecting gear-fixated musician would and fully kitted out my studio for senior year. Alongside my interface, I now had two new shiny sets of pickups bringing the total number of inputs at my disposal to 30, which meant one thing in particular: every microphone and every keyboard in the room was wired for sound at all times. Recording no longer had to be a painstaking process of tracking one instrument at a time, at least not when my friends were around. Now, it was as fun and easy as a jam session. 

My senior year at Carolina was ultimately cut short by COVID, but graduating into an uncertain future came with a few benefits, chief among them the opportunity to decamp to a farm outside of Athens, GA with many of my closest friends where we waited out the worst of it. Over our time there, we made music and CRT sculptures and laid the groundwork for Router. My fellow cofounder Nicholas and I combined our gear into a mega studio, which, at its largest extent, occupied the majority of a floor that doubled as our kitchen. And to top it off, we did another U-Haul trip, this time a one-way trip to California (and sponsored by the company).

## Embedded engineering (no, not that kind)

While I've never enjoyed the amount of crawling necessary to wire a studio for sound, there's a lot about configuring a recording setup that appeals to the design engineer in me. In the years since the farm, the story of my studio has been one of embracing limitations and answering questions such as “how much gear can I fit in a checked bag to Hawaii?” and “at what percentage does a studio apartment become more studio than apartment?” At my current apartment, the studio has once again been given a dedicated room, but that didn’t preclude the necessity of some clever engineering. 

Every home studio owner or residential audiophile has the same arch nemesis: [mains hum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mains_hum), most often caused by a [ground loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_loop_(electricity)). You can try to edit it out of recordings, but that often amounts to lopping off the entire low-frequency spectrum below 60Hz. Treating the root cause necessitates fiddling with the electrical wiring, a difficult remedy in a rental unit. The alternate solution? Running everything out of a single outlet, which is anathema to my aim of having as many concurrent inputs as possible. After battling with a persistent hum in the early recordings I made at my apartment, I went back to the drawing board, preferring to spend my time with acoustic guitars or self-contained grooveboxes. 

The Endeavor presents the ideal chance to reapproach the problem, and a functional studio is a prerequisite for some of the latter items. The solution I’ve settled on: two independent systems running in parallel. A primary system featuring my trusty FireFace, consisting of a plethora of keyboards, synths, and sequencers (plus plenty of open microphone inputs), and a secondary, experimental system powered by a LiveTrak that features the DirtyWave M8 I used for the [URLegy](https://urlegy.com) soundtrack alongside a [patch bay](https://www.perfectcircuit.com/signal/what-is-a-patchbay?srsltid=AfmBOop1NaEdJ4cniS4RW3Bl2FBUTpL0d7uPf_ikfHIIGMT6gv1t-tWr) for my effects pedals.
