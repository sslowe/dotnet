After hinting at my intentions in the original [“Develop a game” post](/posts/develop-a-game), I made good on the foreshadowing and turned *Priority Boarding* into a bonafide product. While design work is inherent in the majority of projects I take on, this was by far the largest exercise in illustrative design I’ve ever tackled. In the past with [Router](/router), I tended to be a downstream consumer of the design artifacts created either by my coworkers or our clients. My focus, on the other hand, was more on function over form. Not that I did’t care about form, just that I knew it was in good hands with Scott and Nicholas. Well, consider this proof positive that I clean up nice, too. 

In any context, design is fundamentally about communication, intelligibility, and the harmony between function and form. As opposed to engineering’s utilitarian concerns (those being "thing work=good” and "thing break=bad”), when design is at its best, it makes clear the very essence of a thing, what it is and how it operates. Bad design, by comparison, obfuscates and obscures. Unfortunately, sometimes bad design is the goal. Ever tried to call your electric company, by chance? As concerns the world of games, design is the thin line that separates a good time from getting into an argument with your friend because the rules he’s reciting from memory aren’t coming across as clearly as he thinks they are. <span className="text-[#77FFE4] text-sm">[No, that’s not an autobiographical example. Why do you ask?]</span>

Rules are a good example, as the rulebook for *Priority Boarding* was one of the more interesting design challenges presented by this task. It’s a necessary yet insufficient skill to communicate your rules clearly. When you’re limited to the real estate of a small bifold booklet meant to fit inside a box of cards, brevity is as critical as clarity, unless you’re planning on including a tiny magnifying glass as part of the package. In the time since my first post about the game, I’ve had the chance to play with more friends (and watch a few play without me), so hopefully I’ve identified any major gaps in my original ruleset, as I also do not intend to include myself with the package in case of questions. Initially, I designed a back cover before wrestling with the copy which turned out to be a mistake when I filled the space inside the bifold and still had several steps to explain, though ultimately, I managed to get all the needed mechanics across. In some respects, writing the rulebook is the ultimate test of a game’s viability, because if it can’t be communicated, there’s no chance it can ever be played.

If you recall from the first *Priority Boarding* post, my initial aim with the “Develop a game” task was to make a game for browser or mobile given my game engine experience from both [Stanford](/posts/what-i-learned-in-slorking-school-is) and Router. It was humorous, then, that much like I would if I was working with three-dimensional models in a game engine, I found myself contending with shapes like this:

That’s the “tuck box” for the game, unfurled into a flat image suitable for printing, which is the same “unwrapping” process used for turning 3D shapes in games into something designers and artists can work with. The first time I can recall coming across an image like that (and promptly thinking I’d never be able to reason my way through illustrating something like that) was as a kid playing early versions of Minecraft when I’d attempt to swap out the character or environmental textures. In fact, Minecraft was the first exposure I ever had to rifling around under the hood on my computer, piling as many mods as I could onto my install until something broke forcing me to start all over in what amounted to fancy copy and pasting.

I would love nothing more than to wrap up (pardon the pun) by show you some beautiful product shots for the game, but sadly, my [MakePlayingCards](https://www.makeplayingcards.com/) order is liable to take up to 30 days. The unit economics are something to behold: from $36.60 for an order quantity of 1 to 5 down to $2.25 if you order 15,000+. So while my little test printing run is not economically viable, per se, I'm simply happy to (soon) have a physical printing that I didn’t scribble by hand for further play testing (or maybe even shopping around to game publishers). If we’re being pedantic, I guess one could argue that fabrication is still pending, but I’d argue I'm just accounting on an accrual basis.
