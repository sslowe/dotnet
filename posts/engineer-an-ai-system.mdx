## Finding the Source

In my time with [Router](https://router.is), AI was both everywhere and nowhere. Over our entire client portfolio, there were only two projects where that particular set of skills came to the fore. The first was [a mural](https://www.router.is/special/breland-mural) for Amazon Music's Breakthrough Artist campaign featuring Breland, intentionally designed as a relic of an incredibly specific point in the development of generative models. I took an off-the-shelf [generative adversarial network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_adversarial_network) that had been trained to produce images in the style of Impressionist landscapes and retrained it (in a Colab notebook no less) on imagery of cities and skylines, with the aim of leaving it stuck somewhere in a semi-real middle ground. The generated output was composited by hand and painted on a building in Williamsburg, Brooklyn. The fact that the initial dataset was composed of hand-painted artworks and the final output was reproduced as a hand-painted mural was by far the best distillation of our name at the time, [recycleReality](https://recyclereality.net). [Ah, GANs. Simpler times, indeed.]

The other opportunity to put my knowledge into practice was less flashy but no less satisfying. Though it never received much of a marketing push (and the original website lives on only in the Router archive), [*RnB Ayo*](https://www.router.is/websites/rnb-ayo) was an exercise in **Good Old-Fashioned AI**, or [GOFAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOFAI) for short. As opposed to modern, neural networks and other statistical approaches, GOFAI relied on symbolic reasoning to solve problems using logic and search algorithms, with applications such as [expert systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expert_system) that encode domain knowledge with “if-then” conditional statements or chess-playing programs like [Deep Blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_Blue_(chess_computer)) that search over possible board states to choose optimal moves. 

It was the latter approach that proved relevant for *Ayo*, and I designed a [minimax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimax) algorithm for [ayo/oware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oware) to pilot the computer opponent in-game. Like chess, the number of potential future game states grows exponentially with each further move you explore. In *Ayo*, if I have one of six possible moves, and then the opponent also has six potential responses, there are 36 possible states for the game to be in two turns in the future. For a game with as many options and turns as *Ayo* (or chess), it's infeasible to extrapolate all the way to the end of the game, especially when considering that it was likely to receive the majority of plays from mobile devices. That meant I needed to design an *evaluation function* that could be used to compare game states that were not binary win/loss outcomes (in *Ayo* you at least have incremental score updates that make the evaluation more robust and less heuristic-based) and not once, but *twice*, because we wanted the AI for each of Benson and Ruger to have a distinct style of play. <span className="text-[#77FFE4] text-sm">[Apologies if this explanation caused any eyes to glaze over. I'm sure my ~~mother~~ editor is having flashbacks to my Stanford-era papers right about now. Let's all remember this moment the next time I want to draw some stick figures, ok?]</span>

That's far from the entirety of AI's impact on Router, however. Because of my background, it was recurring theme in our conversations, particularly when it came to incubator applications. We had a collective interest in tools for creatives like ourselves, and model capabilities were starting to catch up with our ambitions. None of our ideas made it past the concept stage, at least not the AI components, in no small part because of my own hesitation in building a company on top of a closed API like those offered by OpenAI or Anthropic, a necessary compromise for state-of-the-art performance. Besides being bad business sense, the prevailing notions about such companies among the creative class would make a thin wrapper over ChatGPT a hard sell. Suffice it to say, I'm not the only one with a distaste for AI art.

The one prototype that made it across the finish line was the artful fitness app I mentioned in [my post about SPC](/posts/apply-to-spc). We called it [Source](https://src.router.is/), and like any good startup idea, there was the thing we actually built and the vision for what it could become. The beta consisted of a Strava integration that visualized running data by mapping data points like elevation gain to the parameters of a [shader](https://thebookofshaders.com/) canvas. The concept originated with my coworker, Scott, over the course of his marathon training, and was intended as an alternative to the route maps that are the dominant artifact shared within the running community. We envisioned using AI to uncover global connections across these workouts, like how certain music might influence your perception of the activity. While not a runner myself (to put it mildly), I've always been a fan of going for a walk to give my mind room to wander, and I was always intrigued at the thought of giving Source a kind of “situated” brainstorming system that could turn idle thoughts into actionable insights. 

So that's what I built.

## MindWalk: Stretch Your (Mental) Legs

MindWalk consists of two main components: a mobile app that uses iOS's SpeechTranscriber and location services to record and transcribe utterances as they occur along a walk, and a desktop app that receives the data and processes it using a local, open-weight model to segment utterances into discrete “thoughts,” analyze their content for action items and repeated themes, and add them to a global map.

We are just now arriving at the point in the development of AI that I'm excited to build for. Having watched the arc of artificial intelligence over the past decade, one of the clearest patterns is that the state-of-the-art is quickly followed by a flurry of research into ways to make it more efficient, and the capabilities of models that can be run on commodity hardware is finally at a point where you can start to apply them in compelling ways. Sure, they may lag behind the current frontier by about a year or so, but this time last year is when things started to get *really* good. For a project like MindWalk, there is a clear advantage to building local-first, as there is with most creative tools in my estimation. I don't think I need to expound on the benefits of keeping my internal monologue on my personal device rather than uploading it to a third party for training or any other purpose.

Something I found liberating over the course of this project is that MindWalk exists solely for myself at this point in time. Unlike the work I distribute over the web, whether for clients or for fun, there's no standard of polish that I feel compelled to meet. Rather, I'm simply pleased to have it exist as a functional, practical thing that I can continue to tinker on as I use it and see how the system works (or doesn't) for me. I am, however, more than happy to [share the code](https://github.com/sslowe/mindwalk), warts and all, for anyone who would like to try or adapt it for their purposes.
