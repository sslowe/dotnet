One challenge that comes from developing a primary clientele in the music industry is that there is only so much I can do to proactively sell my services. Artists and labels tend to invest in things like [WebQuests](https://quest.carseatheadrest.com/) as part of broader marketing campaigns for album releases, timelines that I have precisely zero control over. That’s why Router’s best client for the past several years has been [Athens Area Urology](https://www.athensareaurology.com/), though we’ve never advertised that fact because of the seeming incongruity with the rest of our brand. We work for them on retainer and cover all their web and marketing needs, which mostly consists of email support for their administrative staff. <span className="text-[#77FFE4] text-sm">[And trying to convince them to make “Dodge the pain at the pump” merchandise every time gas prices surge.]</span>

There’s a certain anxiety that often rears its head in the lulls between music projects that underscores the comparative stability of a retainer model (and may or may not cause people to make long lists to stay busy...). As a means of making those lulls feel slightly less acute while maintaining the flexibility to tackle the weird, creative work as it comes, I wanted to take some of the lessons I’ve learned with AAU and attempt to productize my services for other types of businesses.

[WebHappy](https://webhappy.dev) is a software-with-a-service (SwaS) venture that I’m using to test several theories at once, not least of which is the notion of SwaS in the first place. You might be familiar with its more famous cousin, software-as-a-service (SaaS), a.k.a. the reason everything on the internet feels like a gym membership now. There has been a lot of bluster about the death of SaaS in the face of AI, hence the need for the oh-so-important preposition swap. I maintain that there will always be demand for human expertise, personal touch, and a sense of reputational accountability, thus the impetus in bundling myself as a service alongside the software I provide. My music clients come to me for my vision and creativity above anything else, and the intended audience for WebHappy is one that needs my services as insurance against the large number of “unknown unknowns” on the internet for those lacking the prior experience.

I am also experimenting around the pricing model for WebHappy. I’ve [discussed previously](/posts/design-a-satirical-site) that with AI, the costs associated with software implementation are rapidly decreasing, so with WebHappy, the pricing is based solely on the services offered. There are no compulsory start-up costs for a client to receive a fully custom website, just the standard monthly rate. Those who believe that AI is not going to drive the number of software developers to zero embrace the notion of the *long tail* of software demand, that is, that there are many businesses in need of software that they were previously either unable to afford or otherwise fully unaware of the potential for. Those are the potential customers I hope to identify in offering a high level of performance and design with concierge service at a competitive rate. 

I started piecing together the product offering and visual identity a while ago and launched the website back in July. Within the past week, I started onboarding my first client, so I feel like I can call it well and truly “piloted” at this point.

As I look past the current onboarding process for my first client onto the acquisition of my second, third, and fourth, I’m keeping my eyes peeled for generalizable lessons from this initial trial. One that’s already pretty apparent is that the target customer is likely professional practices, since they are unlikely to be able to justify the effort in becoming the expert themselves and have the funds to warrant paying a third party to take it off their plate. They also frequently have “administrative lynchpins,” operations extraordinaire who are in charge of a disproportionate share of the day-to-day and are particular grateful for the extra support. 

On the other hand, I’m gaining a pretty clear understanding of what is likely to be one of the primary challenges in growing the business, namely how to do outreach and market myself to a clientele that is perhaps less technologically sophisticated, because I’m unlikely to have success with an online-only marketing strategy. I think there could be potential in combining some sort of automated lead generation with data-informed direct outreach, but that’s an experiment for a later day.
