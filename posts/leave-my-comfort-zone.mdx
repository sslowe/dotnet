The result of focusing all my blogramo writing thus far on the Endeavor (well, except for that [*one* post](/posts/i-should-go-there-and-do-that)) is that I’ve developed quite the backlog of non-Endeavor topics I’d like to cover in the meantime. I mentioned my harrowing Outward Bound journey as one example in my [*Survivor* post](/posts/audition-for-survivor), but there are plenty more where that came from. While it has received only the obliquest of references in my prior posting, another topic sure to be rife with humorous anecdotes is my upbringing as a Protestant-raised Catholic schoolboy and all the fascinating juxtapositions that entailed. <span className="text-[#77FFE4] text-sm">[My period of full immersion followed by almost full abstinence from Mass means I’ve never been able to get right with the whole “and with your spirit” business.]</span>

Amongst the most durable lessons (along with the wrong version of the Lord’s Prayer), was the wisdom imparted by my middle school religion teacher, Mrs. Kearney, that growth happens at the edge of your comfort zone – or past it. I know I’m not alone in feeling like I was more willing to step outside of my comfort zone when I was younger. After all, it’s difficult to be rigid about its precise boundaries when your life is characterized by major change and minimal control. As time passes, however, it’s all too easy to fall into the patterns that cause the least amount of friction, whatever that looks like for you. The fact of the matter is that the ability to leave your comfort zone is a muscle and, like any other, comes down to “use it or lose it.”

The contours of what we’re comfortable with are defined primarily by familiarity, the fear of the unknown, and the temptation to fill in the blanks with the worst case scenario. Oftentimes, the worst case is nothing more than a simple “no," and I think that’s what I’m guilty of retreating from the most the past few years. In the early days of [Router](/router), we were mining the breadth of our personal networks for potential leads (and doing a fair bit of cold outreach on top), and given the offbeat nature of our work, we found that the best we could typically hope for was an “I’ll let you know if anything ever comes up.” Worse still was a no after a protracted pitching phase for a prospective project. 

A new era in my professional life warrants a renewed vigor and recommitment to the possibility (or probability) of rejection, though not exclusively (or necessarily) for professional purposes. Something that I spent a lot of time trying to turn into a task for the list, but was never able to find a satisfying approach for, is making a concerted effort at building a musical community here in Brooklyn. I’ve always found music most inspiring and engaging as a group activity, but most of my musically-inclined friends in the city have preexisting projects of their own, so music has been a solo endeavor (little “e") since moving here in 2023. I decided to take a page out of my old pal Cam Champ’s book (who DM’ed me on Facebook the summer before our freshman year at UNC-Chapel Hill) and send some messages out into the ether to try to remedy the situation. It hasn’t materialized yet, but there are indeed prospects of jams on the horizon.

In a similar vein, I’ve been meaning to get around to TR Crandall Guitars in Manhattan to close a decade-long loop. In my [studio post](/posts/set-up-my-studio), I made mention of the [family guitar shop](https://lowevintage.com), and way back in 2014, the year before Lowe Vintage opened its brick-and-mortar storefront, my dad and I paid a visit to TR Crandall’s original location in the East Village. During a family visit to the city for ~~mother~~ editor’s birthday in July, my brother and father met Tom Crandall at his new shop, and he remembered us from twelve years prior. Now I can’t talk shop to the extent that the two of them can, but I’ve certainly acquired more knowledge (and guitars) than your average Joe simply via osmosis. So while I normally wouldn’t make a pilgrimage to a guitar store by myself, I did just that in the spirit of leaving my comfort zone. Besides Tom just being a cool guy to know (I got to see a guitar of Roger Waters they had in the workshop), I’ve also long debated the notion of training as a luthier. If that time ever comes, who better to know than the best luthier in the city?

My final act of comfort zone departure was in service of the next step for *Priority Boarding* (and a future task): scheduling a playtest. I always told myself I wasn’t interested in going to a game store to play *Magic: The Gathering* (see my original [game post](/posts/develop-a-game) for far too much *Magic* discussion), but I figured my neighborhood comic shop would make an ideal playtest venue. As if I needed an excuse to play *Magic*, you best believe that “networking for my playtest” was more than sufficient. Good news on all fronts: it was a lot of fun, and the shop is a viable option (though I’m still investigating alternatives).

If nothing else, this has been a great cure for my frustration at my mostly apartment-bound working routine and proof positive that it’s worth being deliberate about seeking experiences that at first blush seem against type. You never can tell what might lead to something great, though.
